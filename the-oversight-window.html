The Oversight Window

The EU moved the AI Act's high-risk deadline to December 2027. What the organisations using these systems should do with the time.

A practitioner white paper by Julie Hendry. First published July 2026; revised 25 November 2026.

Revision note, 25 November 2026. The July version treated Article 14 as the obligation that organisations using AI have to meet. Article 14 is a design duty on providers. The duties on organisations deploying high-risk AI sit in Article 26, and this version is rebuilt around them. It also separates what the research has established from what is still early, and drops my July claim that the delay was an admission that organisations could not comply. The stated reason was that standards and guidance were not ready.

Executive summary

In May 2026 the European Union agreed to delay the AI Act's high-risk obligations. For standalone high-risk systems, obligations due from 2 August 2026 now apply from 2 December 2027. For AI embedded in regulated products, they move from 2 August 2027 to 2 August 2028.

Many organisations took the delay as relief and stood down. The stated reason for it was that the harmonised standards and guidance needed to comply were not ready. Standards can settle how a system should be built. Whether your own people can oversee it is a question the Act puts to you, and no standard will answer it on your behalf.

The Act splits human oversight in two. The provider must design the system so that people can oversee it (Article 14). The organisation deploying it must use it as the provider's instructions say, and give the oversight to people with the necessary competence, training and authority (Article 26). That second half is about people, and almost nobody measures it. Decades of research on automation give good reason to think a named reviewer is a weaker control than it looks. Newer evidence, still early, suggests that heavy AI use can wear down the judgment the reviewer needs.

What follows sets out what the delay changed, what Article 26 asks of the organisations using these systems, what the evidence does and does not show, and how to use the year that is left.

What changed in Brussels

On 7 May 2026 the Council and Parliament reached provisional agreement on the Digital Omnibus on AI, a package to simplify and re-sequence parts of the AI Act. On 16 June 2026 the European Parliament approved it, and on 29 June 2026 the Council formally adopted it. It was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 and entered into force on 27 July 2026, ahead of the original 2 August 2026 date.

For anyone who owns AI governance, the substance is this. The high-risk obligations, which include the human oversight duties in Articles 14 and 26, move from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 for standalone high-risk systems listed in Annex III, and from 2 August 2027 to 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in products already regulated under Annex I. The Article 50 transparency obligations still apply from 2 August 2026, with a grace period to 2 December 2026 for the machine-readable marking of AI-generated content under Article 50(2).

So the prohibitions and the transparency rules mostly hold. What moved is the high-risk regime, human oversight included.

The misreading

The reflex has been to treat the delay as a reprieve. The deadline that drove every AI governance conversation in the spring dissolved, and with it, apparently, the reason to act.

The risk has not moved with the date. The systems going into hiring, credit, healthcare triage and fraud detection are no safer now than they were in April. The delay was granted for a practical reason: the standards and guidance were unfinished.

When those standards arrive they will help providers build systems that can be overseen. They will not staff your review desk. The part of oversight that cannot be bought with the system is the part the delay gives you time to work on: knowing whether the people you have assigned can do what the system's instructions assume they can.

What the Act asks of the organisation using the system

Article 14 requires that high-risk AI systems are designed so that people can oversee them effectively while they are in use. The people assigned must be enabled to understand the system's capabilities and limits, monitor it and spot anomalies, stay aware of automation bias, interpret its output correctly, decide not to use it or to override it, and stop it. That is the provider's job.

The deployer's duties are in Article 26. Under Article 26(1), the organisation using the system must take appropriate technical and organisational measures to use it in accordance with the provider's instructions for use, which is where the provider's oversight measures reach you. Under Article 26(2), it must assign human oversight to people who have the necessary competence, training and authority, and the necessary support.

Competence is the word to look at. The Act does not say how a deployer establishes it, how it is kept, or how anyone would know if it had gone. You can buy the cleanest override button in the industry, and the control still fails if the person holding it can no longer tell when to press it.

The assumption buried in almost every AI governance plan I have seen is that the experienced professionals already in post will provide the oversight, because they always have. Whether they can is an empirical question, and few organisations have asked it.

What the evidence shows, and what it doesn't

Some of this is settled and some is early. I have kept the two apart.

Settled: people over-rely on automated advice. Automation bias has been documented for decades across aviation, healthcare and industrial settings. People follow automated advice that is wrong, and miss problems the system did not flag. Parasuraman and Manzey's 2010 review found it in experts as well as novices, and found that training alone does not remove it. The Act itself names the problem: overseers must be enabled to remain aware of automation bias (Article 14(4)(b)). Being aware of a bias and being free of it are different things.

Settled, short-term: bias learned from an AI outlasts the AI. In work published in Scientific Reports in 2023, Lucía Vicente and Helena Matute had volunteers perform a simulated medical diagnosis task. One group was assisted by an AI that carried a systematic error, and they went on making that same error after the assistance was taken away. The study tested this within a session. How long the effect lasts is not known.

Observed: a quarter of professionals in one study handed over the whole task. A Harvard Business School working paper on 244 Boston Consulting Group consultants identified three patterns of AI use. Around 60 per cent, the researchers' Cyborgs, worked continuously with the tool and built new AI expertise. About 14 per cent, the Centaurs, used it selectively, deepened their own expertise and achieved the highest accuracy. Roughly 27 per cent, the Self-Automators, delegated entire workflows to the AI and did not build either kind of skill. How people use AI matters more than whether they use it, and the Self-Automators are the people an oversight regime would be relying on.

Reported: the people accountable say they cannot keep up. An IBM Institute for Business Value survey of 2,000 senior technology executives, published in June 2026, found two-thirds of CIOs and CTOs held accountable for AI systems they do not fully control, and 77 per cent of organisations saying AI adoption is already outpacing their governance capability.

Early: whether heavy use erodes judgment over time. In February 2026, Acemoglu, Kong and Ozdaglar published a theoretical model of what they call knowledge collapse: when AI substitutes for human effort, people have less reason to invest in learning, and shared knowledge can erode. It is a model, not a measurement. Klein and Klein, writing in Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, argue that foundational knowledge is what lets a person recognise when an AI output is wrong. The firmest experimental evidence so far is from learners: in a field experiment with nearly a thousand high school maths students, published in PNAS in 2025, Bastani and colleagues found that students given unrestricted access to GPT-4 did better in practice sessions and worse than the control group when the AI was taken away. A version of the tool built with guardrails largely avoided the harm. Whether the same happens to experienced professionals over months of use has not been properly tested.

Put together: the over-reliance is established, the erosion is plausible and early, and in most organisations neither is being measured.

The layer nobody measures

Most AI maturity models measure technology adoption: how much AI an organisation has deployed, how mature its tooling and data foundations are, how widely it has scaled. Those are useful questions about the wrong layer for this obligation. An organisation can score highly on every adoption metric and still be unable to show that the people it has assigned to oversight are competent to do it.

My own research has converged on that second question: whether the people expected to oversee AI can still do it. The evidence base is set out in a preprint on SSRN, which has not been peer reviewed. The short version is that the human layer of AI governance can be measured, and in most organisations it isn't.

What the window is for

The delay leaves about a year before the first high-risk obligations apply. There are two reasons to act inside that year and not at the end of it.

The first is readiness. An organisation that baselines its human oversight capability now, fixes what the baseline shows, and re-measures before December 2027 will arrive holding evidence: this is what our people could do, here is where oversight was real and where it was only on paper, here is what we changed, here is the result. That is a stronger position than any volume of policy documentation.

The second is the baseline itself. If heavy AI use does erode judgment, every month of unmeasured deployment makes a true baseline harder to recover, and an organisation that waits until late 2027 may be measuring an already diminished workforce and calling it normal. If it turns out not to, you will have the evidence to say so. Either way you need the measurement, and the earlier one is worth more.

There is a wider point that outlasts any single regulation. A workforce needs to be able to think independently alongside the machines it works with, including about problems that do not yet have a name. The regulation is the occasion for the work. The capability is the point.

How to use the window

The work divides into three moves: baseline, fix, re-measure. The discipline is in doing them on the people side, with evidence, before the deadline forces a rushed version.

Baselining means assessing oversight capability across the things that determine whether a person can govern a system. The framework I have built for this, CHART, assesses five: cognitive readiness, whether people retain the independent judgment oversight requires; decision architecture, whether the points where a human can intervene are real or decorative; organisational honesty, whether the organisation can tell the difference between oversight that happens and oversight that is merely documented; workforce transition, whether roles and skills are changing fast enough to keep people competent at the task; and accountability architecture, whether anyone would be answerable if an AI-assisted decision caused harm. The framework is openly published. It has not yet been independently validated.

A baseline worth holding up to a regulator looks at the gap between documented governance and observed practice. Does the review ever change anything? Has anyone used the override, and what happened to them when they did? If an AI-assisted decision caused harm tomorrow, who would answer for it under pressure? Those gaps between policy and practice do not show up in an adoption metric.

Fixing what the baseline shows is organisation-specific, but the categories recur. Where oversight is decorative, redesign the decision point so intervention is real. Where heavy users can no longer catch a planted error, rebuild the judgment deliberately. Where accountability is diffuse, name it and make it answerable. Where the workforce is changing without a plan, give it one. This is change work, and it takes most of the time the window provides.

Re-measuring turns the work into evidence. A second baseline that shows movement lets you show a regulator that you measured human oversight, found the gaps, and closed them.

For UK and other readers

If you are reading this from the UK, the EU dates are not your obligation, but the logic applies. The UK's assurance agenda is live, with DSIT convening an AI assurance effort and professional bodies, the BCS among them, shaping what credible assurance looks like. A UK regulator or board will eventually ask the same thing: can the people overseeing these systems do it? An organisation that baselines the human layer now will hold evidence when that question comes.

The same goes for any organisation working across jurisdictions where oversight rules are taking shape. The regulation that names the deadline will differ from place to place. The capability it tests will not.

What to do in the next ninety days

  1. Decide who owns the human layer. Most AI governance has a clear owner for the technology and no owner for whether people can oversee it. Name one.
  2. Read the instructions for use. For each high-risk system you deploy or plan to, find what the provider says about human oversight: who should do it, what they need to know, what they are expected to check. Article 26(1) ties you to that.
  3. Run a baseline on one high-risk use case. One system that matters, assessed for whether oversight is real or documented, while the memory of doing the task without AI still exists to measure against.
  4. Look for the Self-Automators. Find the roles where whole workflows have been handed to AI. They will not appear in any adoption report.
  5. Test one override. Pick a documented human-in-the-loop control and find out whether anyone has ever used it, and what happened when they did.
  6. Write down the baseline and date it. An honest snapshot now cannot be reconstructed later.

None of this needs the standards to be finished, and none of it is wasted if the timeline moves again.

The point

The deadline has moved to December 2027. Organisations that treat the gap as a holiday will arrive as unready as they were in the spring. The ones that use it will have measured whether their people can oversee what they have automated, and acted on the answer.

Being compliant in 2027 matters less than this: when someone asks your people to show they can still govern what they have come to rely on, will the honest answer be yes? That is being decided now.
